Decision

The recommendation, the four moves that implement it, the branch where it flips, and the reasoning behind every score.

Recommendation

Extend now. Hold five years. Sell with term intact.

Strategy E wins the risk-weighted comparison (4.20 out of 5) and is the only strategy in the model with a positive NPV in every expiry case — $134,299 to $494,888 on the central NOI case. It resolves the tenant tail while you still have leverage, keeps the rent and the Proposition 13 wedge for five more years, and sells at the point where a buyer can underwrite five years of firm term instead of a hope.

The decision, in four moves

  1. Find the notice date before anything else. The owner has described a five-year expiry window. The lease has one date. Until that date — and the notice window that precedes it, typically 6 to 12 months — is known, every other decision is provisional. This is the first action and it costs nothing.
  2. Open the extension conversation now, not in 2030. The tenant's own filings show a system closing 50–60 units a year with "block closure" as policy. Your leverage peaks while the site is still one the operator wants. Target a long firm term (5 years minimum, 10 preferred) with CPI or 2%+ escalators, and budget a concession — the model charges $150,000 for it.
  3. If the extension is signed, hold about five years. Collect the rent, keep the Prop 13 wedge (assessed value capped at +2% a year against ~3% assumed market growth, worth about 8.41% of the tax base over the period), and let the market reprice the asset.
  4. Sell with about five years of firm term remaining. That is the deepest buyer pool: long enough to be underwritten as a net lease, short enough that the buyer is not paying for 2035 risk. Then decide whether to 1031, which must be arranged before closing.
The branch that matters. If the tenant will not extend, the answer changes completely: sell within twelve months, while the lease still has years on it. Strategy B — do nothing and let the lease run out — is the worst risk-adjusted outcome in the model and destroys value outright in two of the three expiry cases. "No decision" is a decision here, and it is the expensive one.

Why not simply sell now?

Selling now (strategy A) is not a bad outcome — it is the second-lowest-risk option and it deletes the concentration problem in one transaction. It loses on two counts:

  • It sells at the short-term cap. With nine years of term, the same NOI supports a materially higher price than with three. Selling now locks in whatever term the market can see today.
  • It triggers a one-way tax event. California's transfer taxes and federal capital-gains tax are payable, and the Proposition 13 base is destroyed — the assessed-value cap is a benefit that only exists while you hold. A 1031 exchange can defer the federal gain, but it must be set up before closing and completed inside the IRS's 45/180-day windows.

Selling now is the right answer if you need liquidity, if you want out of a single-tenant risk entirely, or if the tenant refuses to extend. It is the wrong answer if the only reason is that the lease expiry is approaching.

The reasoning behind each score

E · Extend now on a long term, hold five years, sell with term intact

Negotiates the extension now, so the tenant tail is resolved while the owner still has leverage; holds five more years to keep rent and the Prop 13 wedge; then sells with five years of firm term into the deepest part of the buyer pool. Positive NPV in all three expiry cases and the best risk-adjusted result.

C · Hold to expiry and renew then — probability-weighted

Highest expected NPV in two of three expiry cases and keeps the Prop 13 wedge, but the owner spends four to nine more years exposed to a franchisor that is closing 50-60 units a year with no term secured. Roughly a 40% chance of absorbing a vacant year plus a re-tenanting bill. Requires no action, which is exactly the problem.

A · Sell now, at the cap the remaining term deserves

Deletes single-asset and single-tenant concentration in one transaction and prices in the short remaining term. But it sells the same NOI at the short-lease cap, crystallises capital-gains tax unless a 1031 is executed inside the IRS 45/180-day windows, and forfeits the Prop 13 wedge. Financially the weakest of the sensible options; operationally the cleanest.

D · Renew now, then sell in 2028 while the term is still long

Secures the term, then exits almost immediately and gives up the rent stream that the extension was supposed to protect. Only wins when the lease has a very short tail (the 2030 case); negative NPV in the 2032 and 2035 cases.

B · Hold to expiry, tenant leaves, re-tenant and sell in 2035

Combines the tenant's contraction risk with a 12-month vacancy and a re-tenanting bill, then sells the asset just after the market watched it go dark. In the 2032 and 2035 expiry cases this path destroys value even before risk is considered.

Risks that would change the recommendation

  • The tenant refuses to extend → switch to A (sell within twelve months). Do not drift into B.
  • The 10-year Treasury falls materially → cap rates compress, the asset re-rates upward, and holding becomes more attractive (C becomes dominant). The reverse is also true: a 75bp rise costs roughly a year and a half of rent.
  • A catastrophe event → see Risks. Wildfire does not have to reach the parcel to reprice the insurance and the buyer pool across the whole metro.
  • A better use for the capital → a 1031 into diversified net-lease assets converts one tenant's credit into many, which is the cheapest reduction in concentration risk available to you.
  • The expiry turns out to be 2035 → the urgency falls but the reasoning does not: you still want the extension signed while you have leverage, and E is still positive in that case ($134,299).
