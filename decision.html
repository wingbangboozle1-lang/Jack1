Decision brief / Decision paths
Evidence cut-off 7 November 2026

Decision paths

Four paths. One recommendation, with the conditions attached.

Selling, renewing, holding to expiry and exchanging are not ranked by price — they are ranked by what they do to the owner's wealth and options. The ranking below follows from the official data plus one judgement: in a county that is losing population and a tenant that is closing units, term is worth more than price.

Recommendation

1. Renew, then decide — preferred

Secure a long extension at a rent no lower than today, with a creditworthy guaranty, in exchange for defined landlord capital. Then sell the re-rated asset when the owner chooses, not when the lease forces it. Fallback: if the tenant will not extend on acceptable terms by a pre-committed date, sell — and sell to a §1031 buyer if the basis is low. Rejected: holding to expiry with no re-use plan, because a dark box on an entitled parcel in a shrinking county is the worst of the four outcomes.

Path by path

The four options, with what each one costs

Market the property today as a leased NNN asset with roughly 4 to 9 years of remaining term.

Sell now, with the lease in place

Keeps
Liquidity and the ability to redeploy into a diversified or higher-yielding asset; removes single-tenant and single-parcel concentration immediately.
Gives up
The remaining contractual rent, the low Proposition 13 basis, and any option value in a renewal at better terms. Also crystallises the gain.
Costs
City transfer tax of 4.45% or 5.95% of price depending on the band, plus brokerage, legal, loan payoff and defeasance, plus federal and California income tax on the gain.
Best if
The owner needs liquidity or diversification; the basis is high; the remaining term is short enough that buyers price it as a re-tenanting play; or the parcel's alternative use is more valuable than its restaurant use.
Fails if
The sale is priced off a cap rate that assumes a long lease the asset does not have, or the owner has not quantified the transfer tax and the gain.
Verify first
PR-22, PR-21, PR-09, PR-10, C-04, X-03
Negotiate an extension now, at a known rent, with the option to sell the re-rated asset in a later, calmer market.

Renew, then decide later

Keeps
The income stream, the Prop 13 basis, and the ability to sell a longer-lease asset later at a tighter cap rate. Converts an expiring asset into a saleable one.
Gives up
Negotiating leverage at expiry, and some rent - a tenant extending early will want a concession. Locks in a tenant whose brand is contracting.
Costs
Leasing commissions and legal fees; possibly a rent reset; possibly landlord-funded capital (roof, HVAC, drive-thru lane resurfacing) to get the extension signed.
Best if
The tenant will sign 10+ years with a creditworthy guaranty at a rent that still clears the after-tax sale proceeds; the parcel's highest and best use is likely to be restaurant for another decade; the owner does not need liquidity.
Fails if
The extension is signed at a weak rent with a franchisee-only guaranty, or the owner is forced to sell the newly extended asset into the same weak market anyway.
Verify first
T-27, PR-10, PR-09, PR-08, PR-15, PR-19
Collect rent to expiry, then act on the parcel with no tenant constraint.

Hold to expiry and re-tenant or redevelop

Keeps
Maximum flexibility at the end and no negotiation with a contracting tenant. Preserves the option to redevelop if the zoning supports it.
Gives up
Rent between expiry and re-letting. In a county losing population (J-06), the re-letting period is the main risk, and drive-thru entitlements are the scarce asset, not the building.
Costs
Carrying costs while dark (tax, insurance, security), tenant-improvement allowance for a new operator, and possibly a new conditional-use permit or a change of use.
Best if
The parcel has a higher-value alternative use or the site is strong enough that multiple operators would compete for it at expiry.
Fails if
The parcel is a marginal drive-thru box with no alternative use, and the market at expiry is the same or worse.
Verify first
PR-15, PR-11, PR-12, PR-13, PR-19
Sell, and defer the federal gain by buying qualifying replacement real property.

Sell and exchange under IRC §1031

Keeps
Capital in real estate, with the gain deferred rather than taxed. Diversifies out of one tenant and one parcel without a tax bill on exit.
Gives up
Control over timing and choice - the exchange requires identifying and closing on replacement property inside statutory windows. California does not conform to all federal deferral mechanics for state purposes in every case; that needs a tax adviser.
Costs
Qualified intermediary fees, tighter deadlines, and the risk of overpaying for replacement property under time pressure.
Best if
The basis is low, the owner wants to stay in real estate, and a suitable replacement can be identified before the sale.
Fails if
The owner needs cash, or buys a worse asset under deadline pressure.
Verify first
PR-22, X-13, X-15

Pre-commitment

The sell trigger, written down before it is needed

A decision made under pressure at expiry is a bad decision. The owner should write the trigger now, while calm. A workable set:

  1. Renewal test. If the tenant has not signed an extension of at least 10 years, at a rent no lower than today, with a creditworthy guaranty, by [date 30 months before expiry], then the asset is listed for sale.
  2. Insurance test. If a commercial property quote for the parcel exceeds [owner's threshold], or cover cannot be bound on standard terms, then the asset is listed for sale.
  3. Capital test. If required landlord capital at renewal (roof, HVAC, drive-thru, ADA) exceeds [owner's threshold], the sale case is re-run before agreeing.
  4. Personal test. If liquidity is needed inside 36 months, the sale case dominates and the market view is secondary.
  5. Buyer test. If a credible unsolicited offer arrives at or above the after-tax hold value computed on the model page, take it seriously rather than treating it as a floor to negotiate from.
