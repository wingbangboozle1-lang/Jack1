Decision

Sell before expiry, hold to expiry, or renew then decide. Each is priced in risk terms, not in dollars we cannot verify.

Recommendation

OPT-C — Pursue a long-term renewal first, then decide whether to sell the re-rated asset or hold. Sell only after the extension is executed (or, if the tenant refuses a renewal on acceptable terms, sell into the current liquid market rather than holding to expiry).

Why

  • The dominant, known, controllable loss is lease-term decay (R03) — it is the only risk on the list that a renewal both removes and monetises.
  • The dominant uncontrollable risk is cap-rate expansion (R02); a renewal does not remove it, but it makes the asset eligible for the deepest buyer pool, so you are paid for waiting.
  • Selling now (OPT-A) crystallises the shortest WALT of the hold and pays 4.45% transfer tax plus a Prop 13 reset to do it.
  • Holding to expiry without renewing (OPT-B) is the highest-variance path: it keeps tenant-credit risk to the end and then hands you vacancy and re-letting cost.

Options compared

Sell now, before lease-term decay and while the market is liquid OPT-A

Market the asset now on the remaining term (target 2026-2027), accept the lease-term discount, and redeploy proceeds into a diversified or longer-WALT position via a 1031 exchange.

Pros

  • Converts a single-tenant, single-city, single-tenant-credit risk into liquid capital at a known cap rate.
  • Removes R01, R07, R08, R09, R11 exposure immediately; removes R03 entirely.
  • Locks in today's pricing while Treasury yields are near the top of their 2026 range (S09) rather than waiting for further cap-rate expansion.
  • A 1031 exchange defers the federal gain if the 45/180-day rules are met (S24).

Cons

  • Pays the lease-term discount now, i.e. sells the shortest WALT of the whole holding period.
  • Triggers Measure ULA and city transfer tax at 4.45% of price above $5.4M (S23).
  • Resets Proposition 13 base-year value for the buyer, which the buyer prices back into the cap rate (S20).
  • Gives up the option value of a below-market inherited base and of a renewal-driven re-rating.
  • Restarts the 1031 clock: replacement property must be identified in 45 days and acquired in 180 (S24).

Best if: You need liquidity or diversification, or your view is that cap rates keep expanding.  ·  Risks touched: R02, R03, R06, R10, R12

Hold to expiry, do not renew, re-let or redevelop at residual OPT-B

Keep collecting NNN rent through the existing term, then take the building back in 2030-2035 and either re-let to a new operator or redevelop the site.

Pros

  • Maximises contractual income years collected with no sale friction and no transfer tax, no ULA, no Prop 13 reset.
  • Preserves the low Prop 13 base-year value (S20) for the whole hold.
  • Keeps the land and the upside of a re-tenanting or redevelopment in a dense urban core (city density 8,304 persons/sq mi, S05).

Cons

  • You own a declining-WALT asset every year (R03) and cannot sell without taking that discount later, probably deeper.
  • Full exposure to R01/R11 (tenant credit) with no sale to escape it.
  • At expiry you inherit vacancy, tenant-improvement cost, leasing commissions and downtime, and you must fund them yourself.
  • Dense-core Los Angeles rents depend on a restaurant operator that can carry $20/hr+ labour (S19).

Best if: You believe the land value alone exceeds the leased-fee value and you can fund a re-letting.  ·  Risks touched: R01, R03, R07, R09, R11

Negotiate a long renewal first, then sell the re-rated asset (recommended) OPT-C

Approach the tenant now for a 10-year extension (plus options) with CPI escalations and a corporate or franchisee guaranty; once executed and documented, sell on the lengthened term in 2027-2029, or hold the re-rated asset if pricing does not cooperate.

Pros

  • Directly attacks R03: a 10-year extension typically re-rates a single-tenant asset from a 'short-term' to a 'financeable' cap rate, which is the single largest value lever available to you.
  • You keep the sell option the whole time: if the extension is signed and the market pays up, you sell a better asset; if not, you simply do not list it.
  • Because you are not forced to sell, you are not exposed to R10 (credit seizure) inside a fixed window.
  • Preserves optionality on the tax side: you can still 1031 later (S24) or hold for a step-up in basis.
  • Costs you only negotiation time and legal fees; no transfer tax, no ULA, no listing risk until you choose.

Cons

  • You must accept whatever renewal rent the market bears; a tenant with leverage may ask for a rent reduction, TI, or a co-tenancy/termination right.
  • The negotiated term is a documented asset only if the lease amendment is properly executed and the guaranty is real.
  • You carry R01/R11 through the negotiation period.
  • An extension can convert a favourable below-market base into a market-rent reset for property tax if the county treats improvements/new construction as reassessable (S20).

Best if: The tenant wants to stay (a 30+ year operating location usually does) — verify before deciding.  ·  Risks touched: R01, R05, R07, R12

Conditions that would flip the answer

  • Tenant refuses a renewal above X rent → sell now (OPT-A).
  • Tenant refuses any renewal at all → sell now; do not hold to expiry.
  • You need cash within 12 months → sell now and start the 1031 identification immediately.
  • A recession or credit event is clearly underway → do not list; hold OPT-C, because you are not forced to sell.

Weight of evidence

The recommendation rests on two facts you can check in one minute each: (1) lease-term decay is a real, mechanical discount on single-tenant NNN assets, and (2) a renewal is the only lever that removes it without paying transfer tax. Both are evidenced on the Market Pricing and Tax & Rules pages.
