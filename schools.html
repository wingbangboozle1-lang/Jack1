Schools and human capital

School quality is a genuine driver of residential demand and long-run property value. It is a weak driver of a single-tenant drive-thru's rent — but it is a strong driver of what the land is worth to a redeveloper, which is the floor under the lease decision.

What the numbers say

The district is enormous, and that is the point

Los Angeles Unified enrols 408,026 students across 784 schools with 21,246 FTE classroom teachers and 55,601 total staff, on $14.77bn of revenue ($34,533 per student). A district of this scale is an economic anchor in its own right: it is the largest single employer most central-LA neighbourhoods have.

Revenue is 55% state-funded

Federal 19% / state 55% / local 26%. That mix means the district's budget is exposed to Sacramento rather than to local property values, so a property-tax reassessment from a sale does not materially threaten school funding — or, by extension, the local public-sector employment base.

Human capital in the resident base

38.5% of city adults hold a bachelor's degree or higher and 79.6% have a high-school diploma, against a county figure of 36.0% for degrees. That is a mixed catchment: enough graduate households to support premium formats in some pockets, and a large share of households below the poverty line (16.5%) that supports value formats.

What we could not verify — and will not invent

How schools enter the decision

Indirectly, through three channels: (1) residential demand, which supports the retail catchment; (2) the public-sector employment base, which is the most stable employer in the metro; and (3) land value for redevelopment if the unit goes dark. None of these is strong enough to justify holding a lease that the tenant may not renew — but they do mean the downside from vacancy is a re-leasing problem, not a total-loss problem.
